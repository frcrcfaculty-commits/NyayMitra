# Wills, Inheritance, and Succession in India

This page covers what happens to property when someone dies — both **with a Will** (testate succession) and **without one** (intestate succession). It also explains how to **make a valid Will** and the **basic process for collecting an inheritance**.

This is **legal information**, not legal advice. Wills and succession are technical and personal-law-specific. Get a lawyer's advice for actually drafting a Will or claiming an inheritance.

## Two basic situations

### 1. The deceased left a valid Will → testate succession
The Will governs distribution. The executor named in the Will applies for **probate** (in some jurisdictions) or letters of administration with Will annexed, and distributes the assets accordingly.

### 2. The deceased did not leave a Will → intestate succession
The personal law of the deceased governs distribution. **For Hindus**, the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 applies. **For Muslims**, Sharia (Sunni / Shia depending on sect) governs. **For Christians and Parsis**, the Indian Succession Act, 1925.

## Hindu intestate succession — a quick map

The **Hindu Succession Act, 1956** applies to Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, and Jains. The Act distinguishes between **male and female intestate** rules.

### Male Hindu dies intestate — HSA s.8 to s.13

The property devolves on **Class I heirs** first. If none, **Class II**. Class I heirs include:

- son
- daughter (with equal share since the **2005 amendment**)
- widow
- mother
- son and daughter of a **predeceased son**
- son and daughter of a **predeceased daughter**
- and several others (per the schedule).

**Class I heirs share equally** under HSA s.10. The widow takes one share. Each son and each daughter takes one share. The mother takes one share.

If there are no Class I heirs, the property goes to Class II heirs (father, brothers, sisters, etc., in a defined order). If neither, to "agnates" (relatives through males), then "cognates" (relatives through females), then to the State.

### Female Hindu dies intestate — HSA s.15

For a Hindu woman dying intestate, the order of devolution is:

1. **Sons, daughters (including children of a predeceased son or daughter), and husband.**
2. Heirs of the husband.
3. Mother and father.
4. Heirs of the father.
5. Heirs of the mother.

There's a special rule (s.15(2)) for property she **inherited from her parents**: if she has no children, that property goes back to her father's heirs, not her husband's. Similarly, property inherited from her husband or father-in-law (with no children) goes to her husband's heirs.

### The 2005 amendment — coparcenary rights for daughters

Before 2005, daughters had no coparcenary rights in **ancestral Hindu joint family property**. The **Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005** changed this — daughters became **coparceners by birth** in ancestral property, with the same rights and liabilities as sons.

The Supreme Court in *Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma* (2020) clarified:
- the 2005 amendment applies **regardless of whether the father was alive on 9 September 2005** (the date the amendment came into force);
- a daughter has coparcenary rights **by birth**, not by inheritance.

This was a major correction; many transactions in 2005–2020 were done assuming the opposite, and several are now being re-litigated.

## Muslim intestate succession (briefly)

For Muslims, **personal law (Sharia)** governs. The Quran specifies fixed shares ("**fara'id**") for various heirs. Sunni and Shia rules differ. Broadly:

- The widow gets **1/8** if there are children, **1/4** if not (subject to detailed rules).
- The widower gets **1/4** if there are children, **1/2** if not.
- Sons get **twice** the share of daughters.
- Parents, brothers, sisters all may inherit defined shares.
- **A Muslim cannot bequeath more than one-third of his/her estate by Will** — the rest must devolve by Sharia (subject to consent of heirs for variation).

Muslim succession is technical. Get specialist advice.

## Christian / Parsi succession

The **Indian Succession Act, 1925** governs.

- A widow / widower takes **one-third**, children take the remaining **two-thirds equally** (Christian rules, ISA s.33).
- Parsi rules are in ISA s.50–56 with their own pattern.

## How to make a valid Will

A Will is a **declaration of how you want your property distributed** after your death. The basic requirements (under ISA s.63 for non-Muslims, and under personal law for Muslims):

### Valid form

1. **Writing.** A Will should be in writing, though oral Wills (privileged Wills under ISA s.65) are allowed for soldiers and mariners.
2. **Signature of the testator** at the foot or end of the Will.
3. **Two witnesses** must attest the Will. Both witnesses must see the testator sign, and each must sign in the testator's presence.
4. **Sound mind and adulthood** — the testator must be of sound mind and at least 18.

There is **no requirement to register a Will.** Registration is optional under the Registration Act 1908. A registered Will has stronger evidentiary value but an unregistered Will is equally valid if proved.

### Things to include in a Will

- Your **full name, age, address, religion**, and a clear declaration that this is your last Will.
- A statement that this Will **revokes all prior Wills**.
- Identification of **all your assets** — immovable property (with addresses), bank accounts, investments, vehicles, valuables.
- **Legatees** — who gets what, with full names and relationships.
- **Executor** — the person who will carry out the Will. Choose someone trustworthy; preferably younger than you.
- **Provisions for minors** — guardians, trustees if any.
- **Date and place** of execution.
- **Signatures and witness attestations.**

### What a Will cannot do (for Hindus)

- A Hindu **cannot bequeath ancestral coparcenary property** beyond his / her share. The other coparceners' shares are not the testator's to give.
- A Will cannot disinherit certain dependents in a way that leaves them without maintenance — such challenges sometimes succeed under maintenance laws.

### Common mistakes

- **Bequeathing more than you own** — the typical case is bequeathing the entire family home when you only own a 1/3 share.
- **Vague descriptions** — "my second flat" rather than the specific address.
- **Not naming an executor** — the court appoints one, slowing things down.
- **Witnesses who are also legatees** — for safety, witnesses should not benefit from the Will.
- **Leaving the Will where it can be lost or tampered with** — give a copy to your lawyer, your executor, and a safe deposit locker.

## Probate — when is it required?

**Probate** is a court certificate that the Will is genuine and the executor is authorised. Under **ISA s.213**, probate is **mandatory for Wills made by Christians and Parsis, and for Wills relating to immovable property in Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras** (the "Presidency Towns"). For most Hindu Wills outside the Presidency Towns, probate is **not legally required** but is often obtained for clean title.

**Letters of administration** are similar to probate but are issued where there's no executor or where administration is being granted to someone else.

To get probate: file in the **District Court** (or High Court, depending on value), give notice to all heirs, prove the Will (witnesses, attesting documents). It typically takes 6 months to 2 years.

## Practical steps when someone dies

If the deceased had a Will:
1. **Locate the Will.** Often kept with a lawyer, in a bank locker, with the executor.
2. **Get the death certificate.** Multiple original copies.
3. **The executor takes charge** of administration — pays debts, files final tax returns, collects assets.
4. **Probate if required.**
5. **Distribute** as per the Will once debts and taxes are paid.

If the deceased had no Will:
1. **Death certificate.**
2. **Identify all heirs** under the relevant personal law.
3. **Get a Legal Heir Certificate** from the Tahsildar / District Collector — this is the workhorse document for most inheritance claims (banks, mutual funds, insurance).
4. **For property transfer**, you'll typically need a **Succession Certificate** from a District Court (under ISA s.371), particularly for movable property like bank deposits, shares, debts owed to the deceased.
5. **For immovable property**, mutation in revenue records based on the legal heir certificate plus, where required, a partition deed if there are multiple heirs.

## Tax aspects

- **There is no inheritance tax in India** as of 2026. Inheritance is not taxed in the hands of the recipient.
- However, **income from inherited property** (rent, dividends, interest) is taxed normally in the heir's hands.
- If you sell inherited property, **capital gains tax** applies. The **cost of acquisition is the previous owner's** (under Section 49 of the Income Tax Act), and the **period of holding** for indexation purposes is calculated from when the previous owner acquired it.
- **Joint accounts** — survivors get the balance automatically, subject to bank documentation.
- **Insurance** — pays directly to nominees.
- **Mutual funds and shares** — nominees get them, subject to KYC.

## Nominations vs Wills — a critical distinction

A **nominee** is **not necessarily the legal owner** of an asset after the original holder's death. The nominee receives the asset **as a trustee for the legal heirs** — except in specific cases (e.g. shares under the Companies Act, where nomination overrides personal succession laws per several court rulings).

For most assets, **the Will (or intestate rules) governs ownership; the nomination governs receipt.** A nominee who receives, say, a fixed deposit holds it for distribution to the legal heirs.

This is a frequent source of family disputes. Update both your Will **and** your nominations whenever circumstances change.

## When there's a dispute

Common disputes:
- **Will is challenged** as forged, made under undue influence, or made when the testator wasn't of sound mind.
- **Heirs disagree** on distribution under intestate rules.
- **Coparcenary partition** — daughters now have equal rights post-2005.
- **Stridhan** — a Hindu woman's exclusive property — can be claimed back from in-laws after a marriage breakdown or her death.

These disputes go to **Civil Court** (or, in some states, dedicated Family Courts). Get a lawyer.

## Practical advice

- **Make a Will, even if you think you don't need one.** Two witnesses and an hour are all it takes. Update it every 5 years or after major life events (marriage, divorce, birth of a child, large asset purchase).
- **Tell your executor where the Will is.** A Will no one can find may as well not exist.
- **Keep nominations updated.** Bank accounts, FD, mutual fund, insurance, EPF, shares — all of these have nomination forms.
- **For inherited property, get the mutation done.** Mutation in revenue records is what stops boundary and ownership disputes a generation later.
- **Maintain digital copies and digital asset records.** Digital assets (cryptocurrency, online accounts, digital art) require specific provision in the Will. Without instructions, families often lose access entirely.
