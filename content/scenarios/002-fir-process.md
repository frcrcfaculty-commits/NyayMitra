# How to File an FIR (First Information Report)

## What an FIR is

A First Information Report is the document a police officer prepares when they receive information about a **cognisable offence** — that is, an offence for which a police officer can arrest without a warrant and start investigation without prior court approval. Filing an FIR sets the criminal justice process in motion.

Filing an FIR is **not the same** as getting an arrest, a charge sheet, or a conviction. It's the gateway. Without it, the police are not obliged to investigate.

This is **legal information**, not legal advice.

## Your right to have an FIR registered

Under **BNSS Section 173(1)**, where information about a **cognisable offence** is given to an officer in charge of a police station, that officer is **required** to register it as an FIR. The officer cannot refuse on the ground that the matter is not within the station's jurisdiction.

The Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court in *Lalita Kumari v. Government of UP* (2014) held that registration of an FIR is **mandatory** when the information discloses a cognisable offence — a principle that BNSS has substantially carried forward.

### The new BNSS s.173(3) — preliminary inquiry option

**This is a real change from the old CrPC and you should know about it.**

For cognisable offences punishable with **3 years or more but less than 7 years**, BNSS s.173(3) introduces a new option: the officer in charge **may**, with prior permission from a **Deputy Superintendent of Police**, conduct a **preliminary inquiry of up to 14 days** before deciding whether to register the FIR — even where the information discloses a cognisable offence.

Critics, including PRS Legislative Research and several High Courts, have flagged this as a departure from *Lalita Kumari*. The Supreme Court in *Imran Pratapgadhi v. State of Gujarat* (2025) clarified that s.173(3) is an **exception** to s.173(1) — the police can use it for the specified offence band, and it is meant to filter frivolous cases before formal registration.

Practical takeaway: for offences in the 3-to-7-year punishment band, do not be surprised if the police say they need to do a preliminary inquiry. They have statutory backing for this. But (a) it requires written DSP-level approval, (b) it is capped at 14 days, and (c) it does not apply to less serious offences (under 3 years) or more serious ones (7 years and above) — for those, immediate registration remains the rule.

## Zero FIR

**BNSS s.173(1) explicitly allows you to file an FIR at any police station, regardless of where the offence happened.** The receiving station registers it and forwards it to the police station with territorial jurisdiction. This concept, known as a **Zero FIR**, was earlier judicially developed (notably after the 2012 Nirbhaya case and Justice Verma Committee recommendations) and is now codified.

This matters when:
- the offence happened on a journey or in a remote location;
- the local station is hostile, complicit, or distant;
- it's a serious matter (sexual assault, kidnapping) where time is critical.

## How to file — step by step

### 1. Go to a police station
Any police station works for a Zero FIR. If you can, choose one where you feel safe and comfortable describing what happened.

### 2. Give your information
You can provide it **orally or in writing**. If you give it orally, the officer must **reduce it to writing**, **read it back to you**, and have you sign or thumb-impress it (BNSS s.173(1) proviso). Information may now also be given by **electronic communication** under BNSS s.173(1) — but it must be physically signed within 3 days for formal recording.

Cover the basic facts: **what** happened, **when**, **where**, **who** was involved, **what you saw or what was done to you**. Do not exaggerate, do not minimise, do not omit details to "protect" anyone — including yourself.

### 3. Check the FIR carefully before signing
Make sure the section numbers (BNS), the time, and the location are correct. Police often draft FIRs in a hurry and small errors creep in. Once registered, an FIR is hard to amend.

### 4. Get a free copy
**You are entitled, free of cost, to a copy of the FIR immediately on registration.** This is your right; the police cannot demand a fee for it.

### 5. Note the FIR number
The format is typically: `FIR No. [Number] / [Year] of [Police Station Name]`. Note the date and time as well. You'll need this for every subsequent step.

## Special protections for women and vulnerable victims

**BNSS s.173 has specific safeguards** that improve on the old CrPC:

- For offences under **BNS sections 64–71, 74–79, or 124** (rape, sexual offences, acid attack, etc.), the FIR must be recorded **by a woman police officer** or, where one is not available, any **woman officer** (s.173(1) proviso).
- For a victim who is **temporarily or permanently mentally or physically disabled**, the police must record the information **at the residence of the person seeking to report** or another place of their choice, **in the presence of an interpreter or special educator** as needed.
- For victims of sexual offences, the recording must be **videographed** under BNSS s.176(1).

## What to do if the police refuse to register the FIR

This is, sadly, common. You have several escalating options:

### Step 1: Approach the Superintendent of Police (BNSS s.173(4))
Send a **written complaint** to the Superintendent of Police of the district. The SP, if satisfied that the complaint discloses a cognisable offence, **shall** either investigate the case herself or direct an officer subordinate to do so.

Send by registered post **with acknowledgement due**, or by speed post. Keep a copy and the postal receipt.

### Step 2: Approach the Magistrate (BNSS s.175(3))
If the SP also fails to act, you can approach the **Judicial Magistrate** under **BNSS s.175(3)**. The Magistrate, on examining your complaint, can direct the police to register an FIR and investigate. The earlier provision under CrPC s.156(3) is now BNSS s.175(3); the procedure is materially the same.

Recent Supreme Court guidance on s.175(3) requires the Magistrate to record reasons before issuing such a direction and to first call for a report from the police. Be prepared for a short hearing.

### Step 3: File a writ petition
In serious cases, or where the above routes fail, you can file a **writ petition under Article 226** in the High Court for a direction to register an FIR. Generally a remedy of last resort; the court will usually ask whether you exhausted the s.173(4) and s.175(3) remedies first.

### Step 4: Hold the officer accountable — BNS s.199
Under **BNS Section 199** (the successor to IPC s.166A), a public servant who **fails to record information given to them under BNSS s.173(1)** about a cognisable offence is punishable with **rigorous imprisonment for a minimum of 6 months, extendable to 2 years**, plus a fine. This is a real, working provision — and the threat of it sometimes resolves things at the police-station level.

## Other things you should know

- **No fixed time limit for filing** an FIR, but earlier is always stronger evidentially. A delay should be explained in the FIR itself.
- **Language.** The FIR must be in a language you understand. You can ask for it in your regional language; if recorded in another, you can request a translation.
- **An FIR is an *information*, not *evidence***. It is not, by itself, sufficient to convict anyone. Investigation, charge sheet, and trial follow.
- **A false FIR** is itself a criminal offence under **BNS s.248** (false information with intent to cause a public servant to use lawful power to injure another person) and can attract **BNS s.217** (false statement to public servant). Don't fabricate.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Identify whether the offence is cognisable (most serious offences are; check BNS schedule).
- [ ] Go to any police station (Zero FIR is your right).
- [ ] Describe what happened — orally or in writing.
- [ ] Check the FIR for accuracy before signing.
- [ ] Get a free copy and note the FIR number.
- [ ] If refused: written complaint to SP → application to Magistrate under BNSS s.175(3) → writ if necessary.
- [ ] Don't accept "we'll do a preliminary inquiry" as a refusal; ask under what provision (it must be BNSS s.173(3), with DSP approval, capped at 14 days).
