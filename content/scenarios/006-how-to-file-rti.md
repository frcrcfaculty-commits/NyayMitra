# How to File a Right to Information (RTI) Application

The Right to Information Act, 2005 gives every Indian citizen the right to ask any public authority for information it holds, with limited exceptions. The Act has held up — even after the **DPDP Act, 2023** amended one of its exemption clauses — and remains the single most powerful civic accountability tool ordinary citizens have.

This page is **legal information**, not legal advice.

## Who can file
**RTI Section 3** — any citizen of India. You do not need a lawyer. You do not need to give a reason for asking. You do not need to be from the same state, district, or constituency as the public authority.

## Who you can ask

A **"public authority"** under **RTI s.2(h)** means:

- any authority, body, or institution of self-government established by or under the Constitution, by Parliament, by a State Legislature, or by notification of the appropriate Government;
- any body **owned, controlled, or substantially financed** (directly or indirectly) by the Government;
- any non-government organisation **substantially financed** by the Government.

Almost every government office, public-sector bank, public university, municipality, panchayat, and government-aided NGO falls under this. Private companies generally do not — unless their information can be accessed by a public authority under another law (s.2(f) extension).

## What you can ask for

**RTI s.2(f) and s.2(j)** define "information" and "right to information" extremely broadly. You can request:

- records, documents, memos, e-mails, opinions, advices, press releases, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, papers, samples, models;
- data held in electronic form;
- inspection of works, documents, records;
- certified copies of documents;
- certified samples of material;
- information held in any form.

You can ask for things **about other people's matters** (e.g. a list of beneficiaries of a government scheme) — though personal information about identifiable individuals may be exempt under s.8(1)(j) (see below).

## What you cannot ask for — **RTI s.8** exemptions

There are 10 categories of exempt information under s.8(1):

| Clause | What's exempt |
|---|---|
| (a) | Sovereignty, integrity, security, scientific/economic interests of the State |
| (b) | Information forbidden by court / contempt of court |
| (c) | Breach of privilege of Parliament / State Legislature |
| (d) | Commercial confidence, trade secrets, intellectual property (subject to public-interest override) |
| (e) | Information held in fiduciary capacity (subject to public-interest override) |
| (f) | Information received in confidence from a foreign government |
| (g) | Information that may endanger life or physical safety of any person, or identify a confidential source |
| (h) | Information that would impede investigation or prosecution |
| (i) | Cabinet papers (becomes available after the decision is taken) |
| (j) | **Personal information** — see DPDP amendment below |

A key feature: **s.8(2) public-interest override**. Even where information is exempt, a public authority *"may allow access"* if the public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to protected interests. Always invoke this where relevant.

### The DPDP Act 2023 amendment to s.8(1)(j)
Originally, s.8(1)(j) exempted information *"the disclosure of which has no relationship to any public activity or interest, or which would cause unwarranted invasion of the privacy of the individual."* It also had a famous proviso: information that could not be denied to Parliament could not be denied to a citizen.

**The DPDP Act 2023 (s.44(3))** substituted this clause with a much shorter formulation: *"information which relates to personal information"*. This came into force as part of the staggered DPDP commencement.

Civil society organisations have flagged this as a serious narrowing of access. Litigation challenging this amendment is ongoing. Until courts rule, **assume any information that personally identifies an individual may be denied** — but invoke s.8(2) public-interest override and the constitutional right under Article 19(1)(a) wherever justified.

## How to file — step by step

### 1. Identify the public authority and the PIO

Every public authority must designate a **Public Information Officer (PIO)** under **RTI s.5(1)**, and an **Assistant Public Information Officer (APIO)** at the sub-district level under s.5(2). For Central Government bodies, contact details are listed at the **online RTI portal** (`rtionline.gov.in`). For State Government bodies, most states have their own portals.

If you cannot identify the right PIO, you can address the application to *"The Public Information Officer, [Department], [Address]"* and the office is required to forward it to the correct officer (s.5(4)–(5)).

### 2. Draft the application

There is **no prescribed format** in the Act itself, though some authorities have their own forms. Bare requirements:

- in writing or by electronic means (Hindi, English, or the local official language);
- specify the information sought, with as much specificity as you can manage;
- pay the application fee.

A workable template:

> To,
> The Public Information Officer,
> [Department / Office name and address]
>
> Subject: Application under the Right to Information Act, 2005
>
> Sir/Madam,
> Under the provisions of the Right to Information Act, 2005, I request the following information:
>
> 1. [Specific question 1]
> 2. [Specific question 2]
> 3. ...
>
> I am a citizen of India. The application fee of Rs. 10 is enclosed by way of [postal order / IPO / DD / online].
>
> Yours faithfully,
> [Name]
> [Address] [Phone] [Email]
> [Date]

### 3. Pay the fee

- **Central authorities:** Rs. 10 application fee, by Indian Postal Order (IPO), Demand Draft, banker's cheque, electronic payment, or cash against receipt.
- **State authorities:** vary. Some states (e.g. Tamil Nadu) charge Rs. 10; some (e.g. Madhya Pradesh) Rs. 50. Check your state's RTI Rules.
- **BPL applicants** are exempt from the fee under s.7(5), on producing proof.

For **inspection of records**, the first hour is free; thereafter Rs. 5 per fifteen minutes (Central). For **photocopies**, Rs. 2 per A4 page (Central).

### 4. File it

- **Online (Central authorities):** `rtionline.gov.in`
- **By post / hand delivery:** to the PIO. Keep the **postal receipt** and a copy.
- **APIOs at the sub-district / post-office level** can also accept applications.

### 5. The 30-day clock

**RTI s.7(1):** the PIO must respond within **30 days** of receipt. Exceptions:

- where the information concerns the **life or liberty of a person** — **48 hours** (s.7(1) proviso);
- where the application was filed via an APIO — **35 days** (5 extra days);
- where third-party information is involved — up to **40 days** (s.11).

**Failure to respond within the time limit is "deemed refusal"** (s.7(2)) — you can immediately move to the first appeal as if the request was rejected.

## What if you don't get the information

### First appeal — RTI s.19(1)
If you are dissatisfied with the response, or get no response, you can file a **first appeal within 30 days** (extendable for another 30 days for good cause) to the **First Appellate Authority (FAA)** of the same public authority — typically a senior officer designated under s.19. No fee is required.

### Second appeal — RTI s.19(3)
If still dissatisfied, file a **second appeal within 90 days** to the **Central Information Commission (CIC)** for Central authorities, or the **State Information Commission (SIC)** for State authorities. The CIC / SIC has powers under s.18 to inquire into complaints, and under s.19(7) and (8) to issue binding directions, including ordering disclosure.

### Penalty on the PIO — RTI s.20
The CIC / SIC can impose a penalty of **Rs. 250 per day** (up to a maximum of **Rs. 25,000**) on a PIO who:

- refuses an application without reasonable cause;
- gives incorrect, incomplete, or misleading information;
- destroys information that was the subject of an application;
- obstructs the supply of information in any manner.

This is one of the few personal-liability provisions in Indian administrative law, and it has teeth.

## Practical tips

- **Be specific.** "All correspondence between Department X and Department Y on Project Z, between dates A and B" is far more likely to get a response than "all information about Project Z."
- **Number your questions.** Makes the response easier to track and the appeal stronger.
- **Don't ask why.** RTI s.6(2) says you are *not* required to give reasons — and **giving** reasons can sometimes be used against you (e.g. if your reason discloses a private commercial interest, the PIO may invoke s.8(1)(d)).
- **Use multiple specific applications**, not one big one — easier to appeal individual refusals.
- **Keep copies.** Postal receipts, copies of the application, every reply, every appeal.
- **Watch the clock.** Diary your dates: 30 days for the response, 30 days for the first appeal, 90 days for the second appeal.
