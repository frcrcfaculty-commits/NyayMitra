# Domestic Violence: Emergency Steps and Your Legal Rights

> **If you are in immediate danger right now:**
> - **Police emergency: 112**
> - **Women's helpline: 181** (24×7, free)
> - **NALSA legal aid: 15100**
>
> Get to a safe place first. Legal steps come after safety.

This page is **legal information**, not legal advice. If you are facing domestic violence, contact a Protection Officer, an advocate, or your nearest Legal Services Authority — listed at the end of this page.

## The two parallel systems

Indian law gives a survivor of domestic violence **two distinct tracks**, and you can use **both at the same time**:

1. **Civil protection track** — the **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 (DV Act)**, a civil law focused on getting you immediate, practical relief: a protection order, the right to stay in your home, monetary relief, and custody of children.
2. **Criminal track** — under the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS)**, particularly s.85 (cruelty by husband or relatives), s.86 (definition of cruelty), and s.80 (dowry death). Procedure under the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)**.

The DV Act is *additional* to your criminal law remedies. **Section 26** allows you to claim DV Act reliefs in any pending case (including divorce proceedings).

## Who is protected — DV Act s.2(a) and 2(s)

The DV Act protects an **"aggrieved person"** — defined as **"any woman"** who is or has been in a **"domestic relationship"** (s.2(f)) with the respondent and alleges domestic violence. The Act covers women in:

- a marriage, including marriages later found to be void or invalid;
- a relationship in the nature of marriage (live-in relationships);
- a domestic relationship of consanguinity (sisters, daughters);
- a domestic relationship by adoption;
- a joint family.

A 2016 amendment removed the requirement that the respondent be an "adult male", so **female relatives of the husband / male partner** (mother-in-law, sister-in-law, etc.) can be respondents.

The "**shared household**" (s.2(s)) is the home you have lived in at any point, regardless of whether you have title or rights of ownership in it. This is critical for residence orders (see below).

## What counts as domestic violence — DV Act s.3

Section 3 defines domestic violence broadly. It includes any act, omission or conduct that:

- harms or injures or endangers your **health, safety, life, limb, or well-being** — mental or physical;
- causes you **physical abuse, sexual abuse, verbal and emotional abuse, or economic abuse**;
- harasses you for **dowry**;
- threatens you in any of these ways.

The four explicit categories:

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Physical | Beating, slapping, hitting, biting, kicking, punching, pushing, shoving |
| Sexual | Forced sexual contact; conduct that is humiliating or degrading |
| Verbal / emotional | Insults, ridicule, name-calling, threats, repeated demands for dowry |
| Economic | Withholding maintenance, depriving of household items, alienating assets, preventing you from working, taking your wages |

## Emergency steps — what to do, in order

### Step 1: Get to safety
If staying is dangerous, leave. Take what you can — **identity documents, your phone, a few clothes, any cash, your children's documents**. If you cannot take them, do not delay.

### Step 2: Reach out
- **Police: 112** — the police have a duty under DV Act s.5 to inform you of your right to relief.
- **Women's helpline: 181** — connects you to local resources, shelters, counselling.
- **Protection Officer** (PO) — every district has one. The DV Act s.8 mandates this. Find your PO via the District Magistrate's office or `wcd.gov.in` (Ministry of Women and Child Development).
- **Service Provider** (s.10) — registered NGOs and one-stop centres. **Sakhi One Stop Centres** exist in every district under the Mission Shakti scheme.
- **Shelter home** — under **DV Act s.6**, the Magistrate or PO can direct any government-recognised shelter to admit you. The shelter cannot refuse.

### Step 3: Document — but only if it is safe to do so
- Photographs of injuries and damage.
- Medical reports (any government or private hospital — under DV Act s.7, medical facilities shall provide free treatment).
- Audio / video evidence (admissible under BSA s.61–63).
- Copies of WhatsApp / SMS / call logs.
- Names and contact details of witnesses (relatives, neighbours, colleagues).

### Step 4: File the Domestic Incident Report (DIR)
The PO is required to record a **Domestic Incident Report** under **DV Act s.9(b)**, in the form prescribed (Form I of the DV Rules). The DIR is the official record of the incident; copies go to you, the police, and the Magistrate. **You can ask the PO to file the DIR** even if you are not yet sure whether to go to court.

### Step 5: Apply to the Magistrate — DV Act s.12
You — or any person on your behalf, including the PO or a service provider — file an application to the **Magistrate** (Judicial Magistrate of First Class or Metropolitan Magistrate) seeking one or more reliefs.

## The five DV Act reliefs

### 1. Protection order — **s.18**
The Magistrate can prohibit the respondent from:

- committing any further acts of domestic violence;
- entering your place of work, school, or any other place you frequent;
- attempting to communicate with you in any form;
- alienating bank accounts or shared assets;
- causing violence to your relatives or anyone giving you assistance.

### 2. Residence order — **s.17 and s.19**
**Section 17(1)** declares your **right to reside in the shared household** — regardless of whether you have any legal title or ownership in it. The Magistrate can order:

- the respondent to be **removed** from the shared household;
- the respondent to be **restrained** from entering any portion you occupy;
- the respondent to be restrained from alienating, disposing of, or encumbering the shared household;
- alternative accommodation of the **same level** to be provided by the respondent, or rent paid.

> **You do not lose your right to the shared household by leaving it for safety.** Section 17(2) specifically protects against eviction except by procedure established by law.

### 3. Monetary relief — **s.20**
The Magistrate can order the respondent to pay you for:

- loss of earnings;
- medical expenses;
- loss caused by the destruction or removal of property;
- maintenance for you and your children, including in addition to or in lieu of an order for maintenance under **BNSS s.144** (formerly CrPC s.125).

The order can be a lump sum or monthly payments.

### 4. Custody order — **s.21**
The Magistrate can grant **temporary custody of any child or children** to you, with or without specifying visitation arrangements for the respondent. Visitation can be denied if it is not in the child's interest.

### 5. Compensation order — **s.22**
The Magistrate can order **compensation and damages** for injuries — including **mental torture and emotional distress**.

### Ex parte and interim orders — **s.23**
The Magistrate has the power to grant **interim and ex parte orders** based solely on your affidavit, before notice is served on the respondent. This is the mechanism for getting **emergency relief within 24–72 hours** in serious cases. Make sure your application invokes s.23 expressly.

## The criminal track — BNS s.85

**BNS Section 85** (formerly IPC s.498A) makes it a **cognisable, non-bailable offence** for a husband or any relative of a husband to subject a woman to **cruelty**, punishable with imprisonment up to **3 years** and a fine.

**BNS s.86** defines cruelty as:

- any wilful conduct **likely to drive the woman to suicide** or cause **grave injury or danger to life, limb, or health** (mental or physical), **or**
- harassment of the woman with a view to coercing her or any related person to meet an **unlawful demand for any property or valuable security**.

The Supreme Court in *Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar* (2014) directed that arrests in s.498A (now BNS s.85) cases must follow the procedure in **CrPC s.41A** (now **BNSS s.35(3)** — notice of appearance), to prevent automatic arrests. This is still binding.

If the situation involves a **dowry death** within seven years of marriage, **BNS s.80** applies, punishable with imprisonment for **not less than 7 years** which may extend to life.

A criminal complaint can be filed at:
- the police station of the place where the offence occurred;
- the police station of your matrimonial home;
- the police station where you are currently residing — *Rupali Devi v. State of UP* (2019) held that the wife's place of refuge has jurisdiction.

## Maintenance — beyond the DV Act

You can claim maintenance under several parallel laws — and should, because each has different scope and limits:

- **BNSS s.144** (formerly CrPC s.125): summary, fast-track maintenance for a wife (including divorced wife who has not remarried), child, or parents. Available to **all women** regardless of religion.
- **Hindu Marriage Act 1955, s.24** and **s.25** — interim and permanent alimony for Hindu women.
- **Special Marriage Act 1954, s.36** and **s.37** — for civil-marriage couples.
- **DV Act s.20** — as discussed above.

These remedies are **cumulative**, but courts will adjust amounts to avoid double recovery.

## What to expect — timelines and reality

- **Application under s.12 DV Act:** the Magistrate is required by s.12(5) to make an effort to dispose of the application within **60 days** of the first hearing.
- **In practice:** delays are real. **Use s.23 to get interim orders** within the first 1–2 hearings, so you have protection while the main case proceeds.
- **Burden of proof:** the DV Act is civil, so the standard is **preponderance of probabilities**, not beyond reasonable doubt.

## Key resources

- **Police: 112** (national emergency number)
- **Women's helpline: 181** (24×7, free, multi-lingual)
- **NALSA helpline: 15100** (free legal aid)
- **National Commission for Women (NCW) helpline:** 7827170170
- **Sakhi One Stop Centres** — present in every district (`wcd.nic.in`)
- **District Legal Services Authority (DLSA)** — every district court complex

For someone supporting a survivor: do not pressure them to leave or to stay. Listen. Help them with logistics (documents, child care, transport). Let them lead.
