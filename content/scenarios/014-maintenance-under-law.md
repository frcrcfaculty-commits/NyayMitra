# Maintenance Rights: For Wives, Children, and Parents

Indian law gives several groups a right to maintenance — a recurring or lump-sum payment from a person legally obliged to support them. The rules vary by relationship and by the law you invoke. This page explains the four most-used routes and how they fit together.

This is **legal information**, not legal advice. Maintenance amounts are highly fact-sensitive; a lawyer can help you compute and pursue what you're entitled to.

## The four parallel routes

Indian law gives you a choice — or, often, multiple simultaneous routes:

1. **BNSS Section 144** (formerly CrPC s.125) — secular, fast-track, available to all women regardless of religion, plus children and parents.
2. **Personal-law remedies** — Hindu Marriage Act ss.24-25, Special Marriage Act ss.36-37, Indian Divorce Act for Christians, Muslim personal law, etc.
3. **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, s.20** — monetary relief, available to any woman in a domestic relationship.
4. **Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007** — for senior citizens against children and relatives.

These are **cumulative** in principle, but courts adjust amounts to avoid double recovery. You typically pick the most useful route(s) for your situation.

## BNSS Section 144 — the secular fast-track

This is the most-used maintenance provision in India. **It applies regardless of religion.**

### Who can claim under BNSS s.144

Under s.144(1), a Magistrate may order monthly maintenance against a person who has **sufficient means** but **neglects** or **refuses** to maintain:

- his **wife** — including a divorced wife (so long as she has not remarried) (s.144(1)(a));
- his **legitimate or illegitimate minor child**, married or unmarried (s.144(1)(b));
- his **legitimate or illegitimate child** who has attained majority but is **physically or mentally abnormal** and unable to maintain itself (s.144(1)(c));
- his **father or mother** unable to maintain themselves (s.144(1)(d)).

"Wife" under s.144 includes a **divorced wife who has not remarried**. The Supreme Court in *Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum* (1985) held that this applies to Muslim divorced women too — though the law was politically modified by the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986. In *Danial Latifi v. Union of India* (2001), the Supreme Court read down the 1986 Act to preserve substantive maintenance rights.

**Live-in partners.** The Supreme Court has held that a woman in a "relationship in the nature of marriage" (i.e. live-in) can claim maintenance under the DV Act s.20 (see below), and in some cases under s.144 also.

### Procedure

- File an application before the **Judicial Magistrate of First Class** of the district where the husband / father resides, OR where the applicant resides, OR where the husband and applicant last lived together (s.144(2)).
- The Magistrate issues notice to the respondent.
- Hearing — the applicant proves the respondent has means and has neglected/refused to maintain.
- The Magistrate may grant **interim maintenance during pendency** under s.144(2).
- The final order is for **monthly maintenance** plus **expenses of proceedings**.

### Amount

There is **no statutory cap** under BNSS s.144. The Magistrate decides based on:
- the respondent's income and lifestyle;
- the applicant's needs and standard of living;
- the number of dependents;
- existing financial obligations.

Awards typically range from a few thousand rupees per month to ₹50,000+ per month, depending on the income bracket. The Supreme Court in *Rajnesh v. Neha* (2020) issued **guidelines on disclosure of assets and income** in maintenance cases — both parties must file affidavits of assets, income, and liabilities at the threshold.

### Enforcement

If the respondent fails to pay, the Magistrate can:
- issue a **warrant** for the levy of arrears;
- order **imprisonment** for up to 1 month (or until payment) for each month's default (s.144(3) proviso).

### Time limits

A claim for arrears must be made **within one year** of the amount becoming due (s.145).

## Personal law remedies (Hindus, civil marriages, etc.)

### Hindu Marriage Act ss.24 and 25

- **HMA s.24 — Pendente lite maintenance.** During matrimonial proceedings (divorce, judicial separation, restitution of conjugal rights), the court can order interim maintenance and litigation expenses for either spouse.
- **HMA s.25 — Permanent alimony.** At the time of decree, or any time after, the court can order a lump sum or monthly payment for maintenance, having regard to the parties' incomes, conduct, and circumstances.

These cover both **wife and husband** — though wives are the typical recipients.

### Special Marriage Act ss.36 and 37
Equivalent provisions for couples married under the SMA.

### Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956
A Hindu wife is entitled to be maintained by her husband during her lifetime. A widow has rights against the husband's relatives in defined circumstances. A father is bound to maintain his minor children and unmarried daughter.

### Muslim personal law
Under classical Muslim law, the husband is bound to maintain his wife (**nafaqa**). The 1986 Act provides for "fair and reasonable provision" extending beyond the iddat period, as interpreted in *Danial Latifi*.

### Christian and Parsi
Indian Divorce Act, 1869 and Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act, 1936 — provisions for alimony before and after decree.

## DV Act s.20 — monetary relief for women in domestic relationships

If the relationship qualifies as a "**domestic relationship**" under the DV Act (married, formerly married, in a relationship in the nature of marriage, related by blood or adoption), the woman can seek **monetary relief** under s.20 from the Magistrate, including:

- loss of earnings;
- medical expenses;
- losses caused by destruction or damage to property;
- maintenance for herself and her children — in addition to or in lieu of BNSS s.144 maintenance.

The DV Act is broader than s.144 in some ways (covers economic abuse explicitly, allows lump-sum payments), and faster in others (interim ex parte orders under DV s.23). See `/know/domestic-violence-emergency` for the full DV regime.

## Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007

A separate, powerful, often-overlooked law for senior citizens.

### Who can claim
- A **senior citizen** (60+) who is **unable to maintain himself / herself** from his / her own earnings or property.
- A **parent** (any age) who is unable to maintain themselves can claim against children.

### Against whom
- For senior citizens **with children**: against children (and grandchildren, in defined circumstances).
- For senior citizens **without children**: against any **relative** who would inherit their estate.

### What can be claimed
**Up to ₹10,000 per month** under the original Act — though several states have amended their rules to permit higher amounts. This monthly cap is much lower than under BNSS s.144 and is a known weakness of this law.

Apart from money, the Act also provides:
- **Mandatory maintenance from a child** — children who refuse can face imprisonment up to 3 months and a fine.
- **Right to live with dignity in own property** — even a child living with the parent can be evicted in defined circumstances (Tribunal's order, s.23).
- **Reverse-transfer of property** — if a senior citizen has gifted property to a child on a promise of care, the gift can be set aside if the child fails the promise (s.23).

### Procedure
- File before the **Maintenance Tribunal** at the district / subdivision level (presided over by a Sub-Divisional Officer).
- Tribunal must dispose **within 90 days** (extendable once by 30 days).
- **Appeal** to the Appellate Tribunal (presided over by the District Magistrate) within 60 days.

### Free legal representation
The Tribunal proceedings explicitly do not require a lawyer. A senior citizen can appear personally or with the help of any person they nominate. NGO assistance is common.

## Important judicial principles

- **Bare denial is not a defence.** A husband who claims he has no income must produce concrete proof. Courts routinely treat unsupported denials as evasion.
- **Interim orders are common and should be sought early.** Final orders take years; interim orders take weeks. Apply for interim relief at the first hearing.
- **Disclosure under *Rajnesh v. Neha*.** Both parties must file affidavits of income, assets, and liabilities. Failure to disclose can lead to adverse inference.
- **Working wife is not disqualified.** A wife who works can still claim maintenance — the standard is whether she can maintain herself **at the same standard of living** as in the marriage.

## Tax aspects

- Maintenance received under court orders is **not taxable** in the recipient's hands as a general rule (it's not "income" but a transfer of post-tax money).
- For the payer, maintenance paid is **not deductible** from taxable income.
- Lump-sum alimony is similarly not taxable for the recipient (though the amount, if invested, generates taxable returns).

## Practical guidance

### For someone seeking maintenance
- **Document the marriage / relationship.** Marriage certificate, photos, communications, joint accounts.
- **Document the respondent's income.** Salary slips, ITRs, business records, lifestyle evidence, bank statements.
- **Document your needs.** Rent, school fees, medical expenses, children's expenses.
- **File for interim maintenance** at the first hearing — don't wait for the final order.
- **Don't accept token amounts to settle.** A one-time settlement at a low amount can foreclose your future rights, especially in DV / personal-law cases.

### For the respondent
- **Don't ignore notice.** Ex parte orders are common and disastrous.
- **File true affidavit of income.** False affidavits invite adverse inference and contempt.
- **If you genuinely cannot pay**, document it — medical condition, unemployment, dependent parents.
- **Consider a structured settlement.** A lump-sum or staged payment can be cheaper and faster than years of litigation.
