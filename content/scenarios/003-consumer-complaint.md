# Filing a Consumer Complaint

The **Consumer Protection Act, 2019** is the central law. It replaced the older 1986 Act and added important new mechanisms — product liability, e-commerce coverage, the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA), and a faster online complaint route.

This is **legal information**, not legal advice.

## Are you a "consumer"? — CPA s.2(7)

You are a **consumer** if you bought goods or hired services for a **consideration** (paid or promised), and **not for the purpose of resale or commercial use**.

A few important nuances:

- **Self-employed** people who buy goods/services to **earn livelihood** are still consumers (e.g. a tailor buying a sewing machine for her own work).
- A purchase **for purely commercial scaling** (a shop owner buying 50 sewing machines for a factory) is **not** consumer-protected — go to a civil court instead.
- **E-commerce and online services** are explicitly covered (CPA 2019 + Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020).
- **Free services** are typically not covered — but services provided "free" as part of a paid package generally are.

## When you can complain — CPA s.2(11) "deficiency" and s.2(47) "unfair trade practice"

You can file a complaint for any of:

- **Defective goods** — defect in quality, quantity, purity, standard.
- **Deficient service** — failure to provide service that was promised, or providing it in a defective manner. Includes negligence in providing the service.
- **Unfair trade practice** — false claims, misleading advertisements, hidden charges, refusing to give a bill, charging above MRP.
- **Restrictive trade practice** — tying you into buying products you don't want as a condition for what you do want.
- **Hazardous goods or services** — products that pose unsafe risks to the user.
- **Unfair contract** — clauses that cause significant change to the rights of the consumer (this was a new addition under the 2019 Act).

## Pecuniary jurisdiction — **the current limits (as revised in December 2021)**

> **Important.** The original CPA 2019 had higher thresholds (1 crore / 10 crore). These were **superseded by the Consumer Protection (Jurisdiction) Rules, 2021** on **30 December 2021**, which lowered the thresholds to ease load on the National Commission. The Supreme Court upheld the new framework in 2025. **Use these current numbers.**

The pecuniary jurisdiction is determined by the **value of goods or services paid as consideration** — **not** the amount of compensation you're claiming. This is a change from the 1986 regime and a frequent source of confusion.

| Forum | Pecuniary jurisdiction | Where you file |
|---|---|---|
| **District Commission** | Consideration up to **₹50 lakh** | District where the opposite party resides / does business, OR where the cause of action arose, OR where you reside / work |
| **State Commission** | Consideration **above ₹50 lakh and up to ₹2 crore** | State Commission of the same state criteria |
| **National Commission (NCDRC)** | Consideration **above ₹2 crore** | New Delhi |

So: a defective laptop you paid ₹80,000 for → District Commission (consideration is ₹80,000, well under ₹50 lakh), even if you claim ₹5 lakh in compensation.

## Territorial jurisdiction — where to file

Under **CPA s.34(2)**, a District Commission has territorial jurisdiction where:

- the opposite party (or any of multiple opposite parties) **resides** or **carries on business** or has a branch office, or
- the **cause of action**, wholly or in part, arose, or
- **you reside** or **personally work for gain** (this is a new addition in the 2019 Act — you can file from your home district).

This last clause is a major user-friendly improvement. You don't have to travel to a distant city to file just because the company is headquartered there.

## How to file

### Option 1: Online via E-Daakhil (recommended)

The **eDaakhil portal** (`edaakhil.nic.in`) lets you file directly with the appropriate Commission. You'll need:

- Personal details and address.
- Opposite party's details (full company name, registered address, branch office, etc.).
- Detailed complaint — facts in chronological order, what went wrong, what you've already tried.
- **Supporting documents** — bills, receipts, warranty cards, screenshots of communication, photographs of defective goods, the original advertisement.
- The **relief sought** — refund, replacement, compensation, removal of unfair trade practice, etc.
- The **prescribed fee** (see below).

### Option 2: National Consumer Helpline first

Before formal litigation, you can call the **National Consumer Helpline (NCH)**: **1915** (24×7, multi-lingual) or visit `consumerhelpline.gov.in`. The NCH attempts informal resolution by reaching out to the company on your behalf. Many disputes — refunds, replacements, billing errors — get resolved this way without the formality of a Commission filing. **It's free, and it's faster.**

The older NCH numbers (1800-11-4000, 14404) were updated to the single short code **1915** under the integrated grievance system.

### Option 3: In person at the Commission

Walk into the District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission for your district. They'll give you the form. You can do this without a lawyer — the Act explicitly contemplates self-representation.

## Filing fees (current, as per the Consumer Protection (Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions) Rules, 2020)

| Value of goods/services + compensation claimed | Fee at District Commission |
|---|---|
| Up to ₹5 lakh | **No fee** |
| ₹5 lakh to ₹10 lakh | ₹200 |
| ₹10 lakh to ₹20 lakh | ₹400 |
| ₹20 lakh to ₹50 lakh | ₹1,000 |

Higher slabs apply for State and National Commissions. **Senior citizens are exempt** from filing fees in many states. Check the current schedule when you file.

## Time limits

- **You must file within 2 years** of the date the cause of action arose (CPA s.69).
- A delay can be condoned if you show "sufficient cause" — but don't rely on this.
- Once filed, the Commission **shall endeavour** to decide within **3 months** (no testing required) or **5 months** (if testing of goods is required) from the date the opposite party receives notice. In practice, delays are real.

## What happens after filing

1. The Commission issues notice to the opposite party, who must reply within 30 days (extendable by 15 days).
2. The Commission may attempt **mediation** — under CPA s.37, mediation is now formally part of the process (this is one of the 2019 Act's better innovations).
3. If mediation fails, the matter proceeds to evidence and arguments.
4. The Commission passes an order — refund, replacement, compensation, withdrawal of advertisement, corrective advertisement, etc.

## Appeals

- Appeal from **District Commission → State Commission** within 45 days of the order (CPA s.41).
- Appeal from **State Commission → National Commission** within 30 days (CPA s.51).
- Appeal from **National Commission → Supreme Court** within 30 days (CPA s.67), only on substantial questions of law.

A losing party who has been ordered to pay must typically deposit **50% of the awarded amount** before being heard on appeal.

## Product liability (a new and powerful right) — CPA s.83

Under CPA s.83, a manufacturer, product service provider, or product seller can be held liable for harm caused by:

- a manufacturing defect;
- a design defect;
- a deviation from manufacturing specifications;
- not conforming to express warranty;
- failing to provide adequate instructions or warnings.

This is meant to be tougher than the old "deficiency" route. If you've been **injured** by a defective product (medical device, vehicle, appliance, food), product liability is the framework to invoke.

## Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA)

The **CCPA** (`consumeraffairs.nic.in/about-us/central-consumer-protection-authority`) is a regulator created by CPA 2019. It handles class violations — misleading ads, unsafe goods, unfair trade practices affecting consumers as a class. You can complain to CCPA where the issue is systemic (not just your individual loss), and CCPA can order recall, refund, or penalty against the company at scale.

## Practical tips

- **Keep every bill, receipt, and email.** Even ones you're unsure are relevant.
- **Document the defect.** Photos, video, witnessed inspection, third-party expert reports for higher-value disputes.
- **Send a legal notice first.** A well-drafted notice often resolves the issue. The cost-benefit ratio for the company often favours settling over litigating.
- **Don't accept partial settlements that waive your rights**, unless the settlement value clearly outweighs the cost-and-time of pursuing further.
- **Use 1915 / NCH first** for things like e-commerce delivery failures, refund delays, and billing disputes. It's the cheapest, fastest option.
