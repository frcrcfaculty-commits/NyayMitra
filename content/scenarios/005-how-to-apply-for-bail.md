# How to Apply for Bail in India

This page explains the **types of bail** under Indian law, **who can grant which kind**, and **the practical steps** to apply. It is **legal information, not legal advice** — bail decisions turn on the specific facts of each case, and you should engage an advocate.

## What "bail" means

**BNSS Section 2(b)** defines bail as the *release of a person accused or suspected of an offence from the custody of law upon certain conditions*, on execution of a bond or bail bond. Two related terms:

- **Bond** — a personal undertaking, no surety required (BNSS s.2(e)).
- **Bail bond** — an undertaking with surety (BNSS s.2(d)).

The constitutional foundation is **Article 21** (right to life and personal liberty) and **Article 22** (protections on arrest). The Supreme Court has repeated, most recently in *Jalaluddin Khan v. Union of India* (2024), that **"bail is the rule, jail is the exception."**

## Step zero: bailable or non-bailable?

This is the threshold question. Whether an offence is bailable or non-bailable is determined by the **First Schedule of BNSS** for each BNS section. As a rough guide:

- **Bailable** — most offences punishable with up to three years (e.g. simple hurt, public nuisance). Bail is a **matter of right**.
- **Non-bailable** — serious offences (murder, rape, robbery, dowry death, etc.). Bail is **discretionary**.

Look up your specific offence in the BNS First Schedule before assuming.

## The four kinds of bail

### 1. Regular bail in a bailable offence — **BNSS s.478**

When you are arrested for a bailable offence, the **officer in charge of the police station has the power to release you on bail** at the police station itself, on execution of a bail bond. The same right applies when you appear in court.

If you cannot find a surety within **one week** of arrest, s.478 says you are presumed indigent and the court must accept a personal bond (no surety) instead.

**You don't need a lawyer to ask for s.478 bail at a police station.** Tell the duty officer: *"This is a bailable offence under BNS s.[X]. I am willing to furnish bail bond."*

### 2. Regular bail in a non-bailable offence — **BNSS s.480** (Magistrate) and **s.483** (High Court / Sessions Court)

For non-bailable offences, bail is **discretionary**. Section 480 lets a Magistrate grant bail unless:

- there are reasonable grounds for believing the accused is guilty of an offence punishable with death or life imprisonment;
- the offence is cognisable and the accused has been previously convicted of an offence punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment for seven years or more, or has been convicted on two or more occasions of a cognisable offence punishable with three to seven years.

A first proviso to s.480 carves out an exception: a **child, woman, or sick or infirm person** may still be released on bail despite these bars.

For more serious matters, the **Sessions Court or High Court has wider bail powers under BNSS s.483**, including the power to set aside or modify conditions imposed by a Magistrate. For offences triable exclusively by the Sessions Court, or punishable with life imprisonment, the High Court / Sessions Court must give notice to the Public Prosecutor before granting bail (with limited exceptions).

### 3. Anticipatory bail — **BNSS s.482** (pre-arrest)

If you have **reason to believe** you may be arrested for a non-bailable offence, you can apply for anticipatory bail to the **High Court or Sessions Court** under s.482. If granted, the order directs that, in the event of arrest, you shall be released on bail.

Notable changes from CrPC s.438:

- **No statutory list of factors.** Earlier, s.438(1) CrPC listed factors (likelihood of false implication, antecedents, etc.). BNSS s.482 omits these, leaving the matter entirely to judicial discretion.
- **No mandatory hearing of the Public Prosecutor** before grant — though courts in practice still hear the prosecutor.

The Supreme Court in *Sushila Aggarwal v. State (NCT of Delhi)* (2020) held that **anticipatory bail need not be time-bound**; it can extend up to the conclusion of the trial. Always plead this expressly.

**Anticipatory bail is barred** for certain offences — including BNS s.65 (rape in certain aggravated cases) and s.70(2) (gang rape of a woman under 18).

### 4. Default bail (statutory bail) — **BNSS s.187(3)**

If the police fail to file a charge sheet within the prescribed period, you have an **automatic, statutory right to bail**:

- **60 days** for ordinary offences;
- **90 days** for offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment for 10 years or more.

The right arises *the moment* the period expires and you indicate willingness to furnish bail. This is one of the most powerful, under-utilised rights in Indian criminal procedure.

> **Watch out — the BNSS s.187 change.** Under the old CrPC s.167, police custody could only be authorised in the **first 15 days** of arrest. BNSS s.187(3) now allows the 15 days of police custody to be spread **in parts** anywhere within the **first 40 days (ordinary offences)** or **first 60 days (serious offences)**. Critics, including PRS Legislative Research, warn this can lead to **bail being denied during this window** if the police argue further police custody is needed. You should challenge any such second/third remand request strongly.

## **The new BNSS s.479 — undertrial bail** (a major reform)

This is **new in the BNSS** — there is no exact CrPC counterpart for the proactive parts of this provision.

If you are an **undertrial prisoner** (held in custody pending trial), you must be released on bail if:

- **first-time offender** — you have served **one-third** of the maximum sentence prescribed for the alleged offence;
- **other accused** — you have served **one-half** of the maximum sentence.

The provision **does not apply** to offences punishable with **death** or **life imprisonment**, or where multiple cases are pending against the same accused.

A particularly important feature: **s.479 places a duty on the Superintendent of Jail** to apply for bail on behalf of eligible undertrials. You do not have to file the application yourself — though you should ensure the jail authorities have done so.

In 2024, in response to a public-interest litigation, the **Supreme Court directed that s.479 applies retroactively**, including to cases registered before 1 July 2024. This benefits a large number of undertrials currently in custody.

## How to actually file a bail application

You will almost always need an advocate to draft and argue a bail application. The standard structure:

1. **Title:** "Application under Section [478 / 480 / 482 / 483] of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, for grant of [regular / anticipatory] bail."
2. **Court:** the appropriate court (Magistrate / Sessions / High Court).
3. **Cause-title:** State of [X] vs. [your name], FIR No., date, police station, BNS sections invoked.
4. **Facts:** brief, in chronological order. Avoid arguing the merits of guilt or innocence.
5. **Grounds:** legal grounds for bail — false implication, no recovery, parity with co-accused already released, period of custody, age/health, willingness to abide by conditions.
6. **Prayer:** the specific relief sought, including the duration (for anticipatory bail, plead *"till conclusion of trial"* per *Sushila Aggarwal*).
7. **Annexures:** copy of FIR, medical records (if relying on health), employment proof (for surety credibility).

### Typical conditions a court will impose

- Surety / personal bond of a specified amount.
- Surrender of passport.
- Daily / weekly / monthly attendance at the local police station.
- Not to leave the jurisdiction without court permission.
- Not to influence witnesses or tamper with evidence.

**Breach of any condition** can lead to bail being cancelled under BNSS s.483(3) and re-arrest.

## Quick decision tree

- **Are you about to be arrested?** → Anticipatory bail under s.482 (High Court / Sessions Court).
- **Are you in custody for a bailable offence?** → Ask for s.478 bail at the police station first; if refused, apply to the Magistrate.
- **Are you in custody for a non-bailable offence?** → Bail application under s.480 (Magistrate) or s.483 (Sessions / HC).
- **Charge sheet not filed within 60/90 days?** → Default bail under s.187(3).
- **Already an undertrial for a long time?** → Check s.479 thresholds.
