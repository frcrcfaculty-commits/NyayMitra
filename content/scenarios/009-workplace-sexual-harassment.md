# Workplace Sexual Harassment: Your Rights Under the POSH Act

The **Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013** — known as the **POSH Act** — is the primary law that protects women at the workplace. It codified the **Vishaka guidelines** issued by the Supreme Court in *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997).

This page is **legal information**, not legal advice. If you are facing harassment, contact your **Internal Committee**, your nearest Legal Services Authority, or the **National Commission for Women** helpline at **7827170170**.

## What counts as sexual harassment — POSH s.2(n) and s.3

The POSH Act defines sexual harassment broadly. **Section 2(n)** lists any one or more of these unwelcome acts or behaviour:

- physical contact and advances;
- a demand or request for sexual favours;
- making sexually coloured remarks;
- showing pornography;
- any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature.

**Section 3** further specifies circumstances that, if connected to or implicit in any of the above, **also amount to** sexual harassment:

- implied or explicit promise of preferential treatment;
- implied or explicit threat of detrimental treatment;
- implied or explicit threat about your present or future employment;
- interference with your work or creating an intimidating, offensive, or hostile work environment;
- humiliating treatment likely to affect your health or safety.

The phrase **"unwelcome"** is the legal pivot. Conduct does not need to be repeated or severe to qualify — a single unwelcome act of any kind listed in s.2(n) is sufficient.

## Who is protected, where

The POSH Act protects **any woman, of any age**, at the **"workplace"** broadly defined. The workplace includes:

- the office, factory, school, or any establishment where work is performed;
- any place visited by the employee in the course of employment, including transport provided by the employer;
- a dwelling house or house — where domestic workers are employed.

The Act applies whether or not the woman is an employee. **Visitors, contractors, students, patients, customers, interns, and probationers** are all covered. The respondent (the alleged harasser) does not have to be an employee either.

The **Calcutta High Court** in *Dr Malabika Bhattacharjee v. Internal Complaints Committee, Vivekananda College* held that nothing in Section 9 of the Act precludes a same-gender complaint by a woman against another woman.

## Two committees — IC and LC

### Internal Committee (IC) — POSH s.4
**Every workplace with 10 or more employees** must constitute an Internal Committee. The IC must include:

- a **Presiding Officer** who must be a woman employed at a senior level;
- at least **two members** from amongst employees, preferably committed to women's cause or with relevant experience;
- one **external member** from an NGO or association committed to women's cause, or someone familiar with sexual harassment issues;
- **at least 50% of members must be women**.

If your workplace doesn't have an IC, this is itself a violation by the employer (and is a common employer compliance failure).

### Local Committee (LC) — POSH s.6
For **establishments with fewer than 10 employees**, or where the complaint is **against the employer themselves**, the **Local Committee** at the district level handles the complaint. The District Officer (typically the Collector / DM) constitutes the LC.

## Filing a complaint — POSH s.9

You can file a written complaint with the IC (or LC, as applicable) **within 3 months** of the incident — or, in the case of a series of incidents, within 3 months of the last incident. The IC may **extend this by another 3 months** for recorded reasons (e.g. you were unable to file earlier).

If you cannot file a written complaint (illness, intimidation, lack of literacy), the Presiding Officer / IC member is required to render reasonable assistance, including writing the complaint on your behalf.

If the woman is **incapacitated**, the complaint may be filed by:
- her legal heir;
- a relative or friend;
- a co-worker;
- an officer of the National Commission for Women;
- a special educator or guardian;
- any other person, with her written consent.

## What happens after filing — POSH s.10 and s.11

### Step 1 (optional): Conciliation — POSH s.10
**Before** initiating a formal inquiry, the IC may, **at the request of the woman complainant**, attempt to settle the matter through conciliation. Importantly:

- conciliation is at the woman's option, not the employer's;
- **monetary settlement cannot be the basis** of conciliation;
- the IC's role is to facilitate, not to pressure.

If a settlement is reached, the IC records the terms, forwards them to the employer, and ensures both parties comply.

### Step 2: Formal inquiry — POSH s.11
If conciliation isn't requested, fails, or breaks down, the IC proceeds with a formal inquiry. The IC has the **powers of a Civil Court** under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 — for summoning witnesses, examining them on oath, and requiring production of documents (s.11(3)).

**Statutory timelines:**

- The inquiry **shall be completed within 90 days** (s.11(4)). The Delhi and Tripura High Courts have held that this is a strong directory standard but missing the deadline does not automatically invalidate the inquiry.
- The IC submits its **report to the employer within 10 days** of completion (s.13(1)).
- The employer **must act on the recommendations within 60 days** of receiving the report (s.13(3)).

### Interim relief during the inquiry — POSH s.12
Pending inquiry, the IC may recommend, and the employer must implement:

- transferring the complainant or respondent to another workplace;
- granting the complainant up to 3 months of leave (in addition to her regular leave);
- restraining the respondent from reporting on the complainant's work performance or writing her confidential report (the harasser cannot evaluate you);
- any other relief the IC considers necessary.

## Possible outcomes — POSH s.13

If the complaint is **proved**, the IC recommends:

- action against the respondent in line with the company's service rules (warning, suspension, transfer, withholding promotion, demotion, termination);
- deduction from the respondent's salary or wages of compensation payable to the complainant (POSH s.15);
- counselling, community service, or other appropriate steps.

The employer **must** implement these recommendations within 60 days.

If the complaint is **not proved**, the IC recommends no action — but **section 14** is critical: the inability to prove a complaint **does not** by itself mean the complaint was malicious. Penal action against the complainant under s.14 requires a separate IC determination of malicious intent, supported by concrete evidence. This safeguard exists precisely so women are not deterred from filing.

## Confidentiality — POSH s.16

**The contents of the complaint, the inquiry, the identity of the parties and witnesses, and the recommendations are all confidential.** This applies notwithstanding the RTI Act 2005. Breach of confidentiality by anyone with access is itself a violation.

## Appeal — POSH s.18

If you are dissatisfied with the IC's recommendations or with how the employer has implemented them, you can **appeal within 90 days** to the appellate authority specified under the relevant service rules (typically a labour court or industrial tribunal — varies by sector and state).

## Parallel criminal remedy

The POSH Act remedy is **in addition to**, not in place of, criminal law. The same conduct can also be a criminal offence. Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023:

- **BNS s.74** — criminal force on a woman with intent to outrage modesty (formerly IPC s.354).
- **BNS s.75** — sexual harassment, defined to include physical contact and advances, sexually coloured remarks, demand for sexual favours, etc. — punishable with imprisonment up to 3 years (formerly IPC s.354A).
- **BNS s.79** — word, gesture or act intended to insult the modesty of a woman, including stalking and showing pornography (formerly IPC s.509).

Filing a POSH complaint does **not** preclude an FIR for the same incident, and vice versa. In serious cases, file both. See `/know/fir-process` for FIR procedure.

## Special protection: anti-retaliation

**The Act does not allow the employer to victimise, transfer, dismiss, or otherwise retaliate against a complainant** during or after the inquiry. If retaliation occurs, document it and bring it to the IC (or to a court via writ).

## Practical guidance

- **Document everything.** Save emails, messages, calendar invites, screenshots, text messages, witnesses' contact details, dates and times of incidents.
- **Identify the IC.** Every workplace with 10+ employees is required to display the IC's composition and contact details prominently. If you can't find this information, you can ask HR — and the absence of an IC is itself a complaint-worthy compliance failure.
- **Consider whether you want conciliation.** Conciliation can be faster and less stressful, but the harasser has to genuinely accept responsibility and stop. If you don't trust that, go to formal inquiry.
- **Engage a lawyer for the appeal stage.** While the inquiry itself can be navigated without one (and parties cannot bring lawyers into the IC inquiry), the appeal is a court proceeding.
- **Preserve confidentiality but tell at least one trusted person.** Your wellbeing matters more than perfect compliance.

## If you are accused

If you are a respondent, you have the right to:

- be informed of the complaint and given a copy;
- a fair, impartial inquiry with notice of evidence and witnesses;
- present your defence, including witnesses and documents;
- have proceedings in a confidential manner;
- not have your career destroyed for an unproven allegation — section 14 protects against malicious complaints, but only when malice is concretely shown.

You **cannot** retaliate against the complainant. You **cannot** approach witnesses to influence them. Either of these can independently lead to action.
