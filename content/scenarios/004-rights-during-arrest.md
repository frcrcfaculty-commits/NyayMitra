# Your Rights If You Are Arrested in India

This page explains what the law says about your rights during arrest. It is **legal information, not legal advice**. If you have actually been arrested or expect to be, contact a lawyer.

## The big picture in three lines

1. The police cannot arrest you without telling you why.
2. You must be brought before a Magistrate within 24 hours.
3. You have the right to a lawyer and to inform someone you trust.

These are not favours. They are rights guaranteed by **Article 22 of the Constitution** and codified in the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)**, which replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (CrPC) on **1 July 2024**.

## When can the police arrest you without a warrant?

Under **BNSS Section 35**, a police officer can arrest you without a warrant only in defined situations — for example, if you commit a cognisable offence in their presence, or if there is credible information that you have committed a cognisable offence punishable with imprisonment that may extend to seven years (subject to specific conditions in s.35(1)(b)).

For offences punishable with up to three years, **the police are required to first issue a notice under BNSS s.35(3)** (commonly called a "notice of appearance") instead of arresting you, unless arrest is otherwise justified. If you receive such a notice and you comply with it, the police should not arrest you.

> If you are stopped and the officer cannot tell you what offence they suspect, they have not yet arrested you — you can ask whether you are free to go.

## Your rights at the moment of arrest

### 1. The right to know **why** you are being arrested
**BNSS Section 47** requires every police officer arresting a person without warrant to **forthwith** communicate the **full particulars of the offence** for which they are being arrested, or the other grounds for the arrest. This mirrors the constitutional guarantee in **Article 22(1)**.

If the offence is **bailable**, the same section requires the officer to inform you that **you are entitled to be released on bail** and that you may arrange sureties.

### 2. The right to inform a relative or friend
**BNSS Section 48** obliges the arresting officer to **forthwith** give information of the arrest, and the place where you are being held, to a relative, friend, or other person you nominate. The arrest is also entered in an official register at the police station.

### 3. The right to a lawyer during interrogation
**BNSS Section 38** gives you the right to **meet an advocate of your choice during interrogation**, though not throughout the interrogation. This was upheld and expanded by the Supreme Court in *D. K. Basu v. State of West Bengal* (1997) — the *D. K. Basu* guidelines are still the operative judicial baseline and are reflected in BNSS.

If you cannot afford a lawyer, **BNSS Section 341** and **Article 39A** of the Constitution require the State to provide free legal aid through the Legal Services Authority. Contact your **District Legal Services Authority (DLSA)** — every district has one — or the **NALSA helpline 15100**.

### 4. The right against self-incrimination
**Article 20(3)** of the Constitution protects you from being **compelled** to be a witness against yourself. You can decline to answer questions whose answers might incriminate you. This is not the same as a "right to remain silent" in the American sense — Indian law expects you to give your name, address, and identifying details.

### 5. The right to a medical examination
**BNSS Section 53** entitles you to be examined by a registered medical practitioner soon after arrest. A copy of the medical report must be given to you or to a person you nominate. **BNSS Section 56** places a duty on the police to ensure your health and safety while in custody.

### 6. The right not to be restrained more than necessary
**BNSS Section 46** says the officer must not use more restraint than is necessary to prevent escape. Routine handcuffing was held to be unlawful by the Supreme Court in *Prem Shankar Shukla v. Delhi Administration* (1980).

## Time limits — these are hard limits, not guidelines

### The 24-hour rule
**BNSS Section 58** prohibits the police from detaining an arrested person in custody for **more than 24 hours** without a Magistrate's order. The 24 hours **excludes the time required for the journey** from the place of arrest to the Magistrate's court.

This is the same rule as **Article 22(2)** of the Constitution. It is non-negotiable. If the police hold you longer without producing you before a Magistrate, your detention becomes illegal and you can be released by way of a *habeas corpus* petition.

### After 24 hours: police custody vs judicial custody
Once you are produced before a Magistrate, the Magistrate can authorise **further detention under BNSS Section 187**. The Magistrate may authorise:

- up to **15 days** of custody at one stretch (in whole or in parts);
- **total custody up to 60 days** for ordinary offences, or **up to 90 days** where the investigation relates to an offence punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment for 10 years or more.

If the police do not file a charge sheet within 60/90 days, you have a **statutory right to default bail** under s.187(3) of BNSS, provided you are willing to furnish bail.

> A particular point of attention with BNSS s.187: the 15 days of police custody can now be authorised in **whole or in parts** at any point during the **first 40 or 60 days** of the detention period. Earlier, under CrPC s.167, police custody had to be in the first 15 days only. This change has been flagged by PRS Legislative Research and several legal commentators as having implications for bail.

## What you should do, practically

If you or someone you know is being arrested:

- **Stay calm. Do not resist.** Resistance can lead to additional charges under BNS s.121 (assault on public servant).
- **Ask the officer for the grounds of arrest** (s.47) and the **memo of arrest** (the official record). The Supreme Court in *D. K. Basu* required the memo to be attested by at least one witness (a family member or a respectable local person) and countersigned by you.
- **Note the officer's name, rank, and police station.** The Supreme Court in *Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar* (2014) directed that police must record reasons for arrest in writing for offences punishable with up to seven years; BNSS s.35 carries this forward.
- **Ask the officer to inform a specific relative or friend** (s.48). If you can, give the officer the phone number.
- **Ask to call a lawyer** before you sign anything. Do not sign blank papers.
- **If you are taken to a hospital**, ask for a copy of the medical report (s.53).
- **Keep track of the time of arrest.** The 24-hour clock begins then.

## What if your rights are violated?

- **Habeas corpus petition** — file in the High Court under Article 226 (or in the Supreme Court under Article 32) if you are being held illegally.
- **Compensation** — *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) and *D. K. Basu* establish that the State pays monetary compensation for illegal detention.
- **Complaint to the State Human Rights Commission or NHRC** — for custodial violence or torture.
- **Internal departmental complaint** — to the Superintendent of Police of the district, with copy to the State Human Rights Commission.
