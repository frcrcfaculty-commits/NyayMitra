# Dowry Prohibition: What's Illegal, What to Do

> **If you are facing dowry harassment now**, dial **181** (women's helpline), **7827170170** (National Commission for Women), or **15100** (NALSA legal aid). For an immediate threat to life, dial **112**.

This is **legal information**, not legal advice.

## What "dowry" means in law — DPA s.2

Under **Section 2 of the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961**, "dowry" means:

> any property or valuable security given or agreed to be given either directly or indirectly:
> (a) by one party to the marriage to the other party to the marriage; or
> (b) by the parents of either party, or by any other person, to either party or to any other person,
> at, or before, or any time after the marriage, in connection with the marriage of the said parties.

Crucially, this **excludes "dower" or "mahr"** in Muslim law and **gifts given to the bride which she keeps as her own property** (such gifts are her *stridhan* — see below).

The phrase "**in connection with the marriage**" is the key. Demands made *because of* the marriage relationship — even years later, even framed as "investment for the family's prestige" — fall within dowry. The Supreme Court in *S. Gopal Reddy v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (1996) held that the Act targets both **giving and demanding** dowry.

## The four offences under the Dowry Prohibition Act

### Section 3 — Giving or taking dowry
**Both giver and taker are punishable.**
- Imprisonment **not less than 5 years**, plus fine of **₹15,000** or the value of the dowry, whichever is more.
- Cognisable, non-bailable, non-compoundable.

The Act criminalises giving as well as taking — though prosecutions of givers (typically the bride's family) have been rare in practice, both because they are also victims and because of evidentiary challenges.

### Section 4 — Demanding dowry
- Direct or indirect demand from a party to the marriage, parents, or guardians.
- Imprisonment **not less than 6 months**, extendable to 2 years, plus fine up to **₹10,000**.
- Cognisable, non-bailable, non-compoundable.

### Section 4A — Advertising dowry
Banning ads that offer money, share, or property as consideration for the marriage.

### Section 6 — Failure to transfer dowry to the woman
**This provision is often overlooked, and it's powerful.** Any dowry received by anyone other than the woman in connection with whose marriage it was given **must be transferred to her** within prescribed periods. If transferred to her parents-in-law, mother-in-law, etc., they hold it **in trust** for her.

Failure to transfer attracts imprisonment of 6 months to 2 years, plus fine of ₹5,000–₹10,000, plus an order to transfer.

If she dies before transfer:
- **with children**: dowry transfers to her children;
- **without children**: dowry transfers to her parents.

In a dowry death case, the failure-to-transfer offence is often a relevant additional charge.

### Section 7 — Cognisance and offences
Offences under the Act are cognisable for the purpose of investigation and arrest, and **any police officer not below the rank of Sub-Inspector** can investigate.

### Section 8 — Burden of proof
For offences under sections 3 and 4, **the burden of proof shifts to the accused** to show that they have **not committed** the offence. This is unusual for criminal statutes and reflects the legislative intent to take dowry seriously.

## Dowry death — BNS Section 80

**Section 80 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023** (formerly IPC s.304B) defines and punishes dowry death.

### Definition (s.80(1))
A death is a "dowry death" if **all** of the following are true:

1. The death of a **woman** is caused by **burns**, **bodily injury**, or **otherwise than under normal circumstances**;
2. The death occurs **within 7 years of the marriage**;
3. **Soon before her death** she was subjected to **cruelty or harassment** by her **husband or any relative of her husband**;
4. The cruelty / harassment was **for, or in connection with, any demand for dowry**.

Where these conditions are met, the husband or relative is **deemed to have caused her death**. The deeming is automatic — the prosecution does not have to prove that any specific person physically caused the death.

### Punishment (s.80(2))
Imprisonment **not less than 7 years**, extendable to **life imprisonment**.

### Presumption — BSA s.118
**Section 118 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023** (formerly Indian Evidence Act s.113B) creates a **rebuttable presumption** that, when the basic ingredients of s.80 are shown, **the accused has caused the dowry death**. The burden is then on the accused to rebut the presumption. This presumption is one of the strongest pro-victim evidentiary tools in Indian criminal law.

## Cruelty — BNS Section 85 and 86

**BNS s.85** (formerly IPC s.498A) makes it a criminal offence for a **husband or any relative of a husband** to subject a married woman to **cruelty**.

- **Punishment:** imprisonment up to **3 years** plus fine.
- **Cognisable, non-bailable.**

**BNS s.86** defines "cruelty" for s.85 as:

(a) any **wilful conduct** which is of such a nature as is likely to **drive the woman to commit suicide** or to cause **grave injury or danger to life, limb or health** (whether mental or physical), or

(b) **harassment of the woman** with a view to coercing her or any person related to her to meet **any unlawful demand for any property or valuable security**, or on account of failure by her or any related person to meet such demand.

So s.85 covers both general cruelty and dowry-related harassment.

### *Arnesh Kumar* safeguard

In *Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar* (2014) — and now reinforced under BNSS s.35(3) — the Supreme Court directed that arrests under s.498A (now BNS s.85) **cannot be automatic**. Police must:

- record reasons in writing for the arrest;
- comply with BNSS s.35 conditions before arresting;
- consider whether issuing a notice of appearance under BNSS s.35(3) is sufficient instead of arrest.

This was meant to balance the genuine grievances of women with concerns about misuse of s.498A by some complainants. Both concerns are real; the *Arnesh Kumar* framework is the working compromise.

## What to do if you're facing dowry harassment

### Immediate steps
1. **Document.** Specific dates, demands, amounts. WhatsApp messages, emails, voice notes (kept securely on your own phone or cloud — not just on devices the harasser controls).
2. **Photograph injuries** if any, and seek medical care from a hospital — get medical reports.
3. **Get to safety** if there's a physical threat.
4. **Inform a trusted person** — parents, sibling, friend, NGO — about what's happening.

### Helplines
- **Women's helpline: 181** (24×7, free, multi-lingual)
- **National Commission for Women: 7827170170**
- **NALSA legal aid: 15100**
- **Police emergency: 112**
- **Sakhi One Stop Centre** — every district has one under Mission Shakti.

### Legal options (you can choose more than one)

**Option A: FIR under BNS s.85 + DPA**
File at any police station (Zero FIR — see `/know/fir-process`). The complaint should specify:
- the marriage details;
- specific dowry demands with dates / amounts where known;
- specific instances of cruelty (mental, physical, harassment);
- relevant sections: BNS s.85, DPA ss.3 / 4 / 6, and (if relevant) BNS s.80 if a dowry death has occurred.

**Option B: DV Act application**
Get a Protection Officer / Magistrate to issue:
- protection order (s.18);
- residence order (s.17/19);
- monetary relief (s.20);
- custody (s.21) / compensation (s.22).
See `/know/domestic-violence-emergency`.

**Option C: Maintenance under BNSS s.144**
For ongoing financial support. See `/know/maintenance-under-law`.

**Option D: Divorce / judicial separation**
Cruelty (including dowry harassment) is a ground under HMA s.13(1)(ia), SMA s.27, etc.

These can be pursued **in parallel**.

## *Stridhan* — your own property

**Stridhan** is property given to a woman before, during, or after marriage that is **her absolute property**. It typically includes:

- gifts given at marriage (jewellery, clothes, household goods) where the donor intended them as gifts to her;
- gifts from her parents at any time;
- her own earnings;
- gifts from the husband and his relatives to her personally.

*Stridhan* is **not dowry** and is **not** subject to DPA s.6. It is hers absolutely. **In-laws holding her *stridhan* are holding it in trust** and must return it on demand. Refusal is criminal misappropriation under BNS s.314 and breach of trust under BNS s.316.

If you're leaving an in-law household, list and reclaim your *stridhan* — jewellery, gifts, personal items. Make a list and serve it on the in-laws if necessary; failure to return triggers criminal liability.

## Concerns about misuse — and the safeguards

The Supreme Court has repeatedly observed that some s.498A complaints (now BNS s.85) involve over-implication of distant relatives or are filed in retaliation. Important safeguards now in place:

- ***Arnesh Kumar* checklist** — police cannot arrest reflexively.
- ***Rajesh Sharma v. State of UP* (2017)** initially set up Family Welfare Committees to vet complaints — substantially modified by *Social Action Forum for Manav Adhikar v. UOI* (2018), which struck down the FWC framework as judicial legislation, while retaining the *Arnesh Kumar* safeguards.
- ***Achin Gupta v. State of Haryana* (2024)** and several others — courts increasingly scrutinise vague allegations against in-laws living separately.

These safeguards do not weaken genuine claims. They aim to prevent abuse of the law. The remedy for both — for the genuine victim and the falsely accused — is **specific, documented, evidentially-grounded** complaints and defences.

## If you have been falsely accused

- **Take notice seriously.** Engage a lawyer immediately.
- **Apply for anticipatory bail** under BNSS s.482 — see `/know/how-to-apply-for-bail`.
- **Document your defence.** Alibi (you were not present), lack of proximate link to cruelty / death, joint family separation evidence.
- **Quashing under BNSS s.528** (formerly CrPC s.482) is possible where the FIR fails to disclose an offence on its face — but courts apply this carefully.
