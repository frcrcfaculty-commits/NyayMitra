# Tenant Rights in India

> **Important.** Tenant–landlord law in India is largely **state-specific**. What's true in Maharashtra under the Maharashtra Rent Control Act, 1999 may differ from Delhi under the Delhi Rent Act, 1995, or from Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, etc. This page explains the **general framework** and the most common state-level patterns, but you must check your **state's Rent Control Act** for the binding rules where you live.

This is **legal information**, not legal advice.

## Where the rules come from

Three layers of law govern your tenancy:

1. **Your rent agreement (lease).** This is the primary source of your specific rights and obligations. Read it.
2. **Your State Rent Control Act.** Almost every state has one. Examples: Maharashtra Rent Control Act 1999, Delhi Rent Act 1995, Karnataka Rent Act 1999, Tamil Nadu Regulation of Rights and Responsibilities of Landlords and Tenants Act 2017.
3. **Background central law.** The **Transfer of Property Act, 1882** (especially Section 108 — rights and liabilities of lessor and lessee) and the **Indian Contract Act, 1872** apply unless a state law overrides them.

The **Model Tenancy Act, 2021** is a *model law* the Central Government has circulated for states to adopt. **It is binding only in states that have notified it.** As of early 2026, only a handful of states / UTs have done so. Do not assume the MTA applies in your state — check.

## Security deposit

**There is no nationwide cap.** What's allowed depends on your state and your agreement.

Common patterns:

- **Where the Model Tenancy Act 2021 applies** — capped at **2 months' rent** for residential premises, 6 months for non-residential. (Section 11 of the MTA.)
- **Maharashtra** — under the MRC Act, deposits of **3 months' rent** are common; some agreements ask for more. The Bombay High Court has, in several cases, treated deposits beyond 6 months as unconscionable.
- **Bengaluru / Karnataka practice** — informal market practice runs to **10 months' rent**, despite state law and judicial discomfort with this.
- **Delhi** — the Delhi Rent Act 1995 doesn't cap deposits explicitly; the agreement governs.

**Refund.** The deposit is your money. The landlord can deduct only for **legitimate damages beyond normal wear and tear**, and is required to give you an **itemised account** of any deductions. Where the MTA applies, the refund window is **1 month after vacating**. Elsewhere, it's whatever your agreement says — and if the agreement is silent, "within reasonable time" under the TPA.

## Rent increase

**Cannot be unilateral.** Any rent increase must be either (a) provided for in your rent agreement, or (b) within limits set by your state's Rent Control Act.

- **MTA states** — landlord must give 3 months' written notice before any increase (s.9).
- **Maharashtra (MRC Act)** — annual permissible increase capped at percentages set by the state, varying by tenancy type.
- **Many states** — the agreement-stipulated percentage governs, often 5–10% annually.

If your landlord raises rent outside what the agreement and state law allow, you can refuse to pay the excess.

## Eviction protection

You **cannot** be evicted by self-help. The landlord must follow due process:

1. **Valid grounds.** State Rent Control Acts list permissible grounds: non-payment of rent, breach of agreement, sub-letting without consent, unlawful use, landlord's bona-fide need for own occupation, etc.
2. **Written notice.** The landlord must serve a written notice. The duration depends on state law and your tenancy type — 15 days, 30 days, or 3 months are common.
3. **Court order.** Only a **Civil Court / Rent Controller / Small Causes Court** (depending on state) can order eviction. Police cannot evict you on a landlord's say-so.

### Things the landlord cannot do

- **Cut off essential services** — water, electricity, gas. This has been held by courts to be coercive and unlawful, and many state Rent Control Acts treat it as a specific offence. Disconnecting essentials to force eviction can also constitute criminal intimidation or mischief under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.
- **Forcibly remove you or your belongings.**
- **Change the locks while you're out.**
- **Threaten you, your family, or visitors.**

These acts are **illegal regardless of whether you owe rent**. Owed rent is recovered through court, not through pressure tactics.

## When your fixed-term lease ends

Two things can happen:

1. **The lease is renewed** (often with a small rent increase per the agreement).
2. **The lease ends** and either party serves notice. Under TPA s.111, a lease ends by efflux of time, by notice to quit (for periodic tenancies), by forfeiture (for breach), or by other specified means.

**Important:** if you continue to pay rent and the landlord accepts it after the lease ends, you become a **tenant by holding over** under TPA s.116 — which has narrower protections than a fresh lease. Sign a renewal in writing.

## Your maintenance obligations

You are typically responsible for:
- paying rent on time;
- not damaging the property beyond normal wear;
- paying utilities you contracted for;
- not sub-letting without written landlord consent;
- giving the landlord access for inspection on reasonable notice;
- vacating peacefully at lease end.

The landlord is responsible for **major structural repairs** — leaks, structural cracks, faulty wiring, plumbing — unless the agreement explicitly shifts these to you.

## What to do if your rights are violated

1. **Document.** Keep your rent agreement, every receipt (rent and deposit), and all communications. Photograph the condition of the property at move-in and move-out.
2. **Send a written legal notice.** Through an advocate, or in your own name if small. A formal notice often resolves the dispute without litigation.
3. **Approach the Rent Controller / Civil Court.** Each state has a Rent Controller (or designated Civil Court / Small Causes Court) for disputes.
4. **Police complaint** — only for criminal conduct (cut-off services, threats, forcible entry). For pure civil disputes (rent, deposit refund), police will not, and should not, intervene.
5. **Free legal aid.** Call **NALSA helpline 15100** or visit your **District Legal Services Authority (DLSA)** for free advice and representation if eligible.

## Practical advice when signing a lease

- **Get the agreement registered** if rent + deposit + duration crosses state-specific thresholds (typically: leases >11 months must be registered under the Registration Act 1908). The "11-month renewable" lease is a workaround to avoid registration; it does not strip you of TPA rights but it does mean you need a fresh document each year.
- **Get rent receipts** with the landlord's signature, every month. PAN of the landlord is required if annual rent exceeds Rs. 1,00,000 (for HRA claims).
- **Photograph the property** at move-in. Make a written inventory of any existing damage and have the landlord countersign. This protects your deposit later.
- **Read the deposit clause carefully.** Look for: amount, conditions for forfeiture, refund timeline, what counts as "damage."
- **Be wary of:** clauses saying the deposit is non-refundable in any circumstance; clauses giving the landlord unilateral termination rights without notice; clauses banning visitors.
