# Cheque Bounce: Your Rights and Remedies

A bounced cheque is one of the most common legal issues in India. The good news: **Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881** gives the payee a fast-track quasi-criminal remedy with strong presumptions in their favour.

This is **legal information**, not legal advice.

## What "cheque bounce" means

A cheque is dishonoured when the bank refuses to pay it. Common reasons printed on the bank's return memo:

- **Insufficient funds** (or "funds insufficient")
- **Exceeds arrangement** (the cheque is more than the agreed overdraft limit)
- **Account closed** / "account does not exist"
- **Stop payment** by the drawer
- **Signature differs**
- **Cheque post-dated / stale-dated**

**Section 138 of the NI Act applies primarily in two situations:** when the cheque is returned for **insufficient funds** or because **the amount exceeds the arrangement** with the bank. The Supreme Court has interpreted s.138 to cover most "drawer-fault" returns — including stop-payment after the cheque was issued (*Modi Cements v. Kuchil Kumar Nandi*, 1998).

If the bank dishonours due to **bank-side error**, that is not a s.138 offence — but the drawer may still be liable in civil recovery.

## What makes Section 138 a criminal offence

For s.138 to apply, **all** these must be true:

1. The cheque was drawn by the accused on his/her account for payment of a **legally enforceable debt or liability**;
2. The cheque was presented to the bank within **3 months** of its date (or its validity period, whichever is earlier — the RBI reduced cheque validity from 6 months to 3 months in 2012);
3. The cheque was returned unpaid by the bank for insufficiency of funds or exceeding the arranged amount;
4. The payee gave a **written demand notice** to the drawer **within 30 days** of receiving the bank's return memo;
5. The drawer failed to pay the cheque amount **within 15 days** of receiving the demand notice.

If all five conditions are met, the offence is complete.

## The presumption — NI Act s.139

This is the single most important thing to know about cheque bounce cases. **Section 139 creates a presumption that the cheque was issued for the discharge of a debt or liability.** Once the payee proves the cheque was signed and bounced, the **burden shifts to the drawer** to prove that there was no underlying debt.

This is unusual — in most criminal law, the prosecution proves everything. In s.138 cases, the drawer has to actively rebut.

## The drawer's defences

A drawer can rebut the s.139 presumption by showing, on a **preponderance of probabilities** (not beyond reasonable doubt):

- the cheque was a **security cheque** that wasn't meant to be encashed in those circumstances;
- the cheque was **lost or stolen** and misused;
- the **alleged debt does not exist** — e.g. the loan was never given, or has been repaid, or the amount stated in the cheque exceeds what was owed;
- the cheque was issued under **coercion or undue influence**;
- the cheque was **post-dated** and presented before the date.

Mere assertion is not enough. The drawer must usually produce documentary or testimonial evidence to rebut the presumption.

## Step-by-step process for the payee

### Step 1: Get the bank return memo
When your bank returns the cheque, you receive a **memo of dishonour** stating the reason. **Keep this safely.** It is the trigger document for the entire process.

### Step 2: Issue the legal notice — within 30 days
Draft and send a **demand notice** to the drawer **within 30 days** of receiving the return memo, demanding payment within 15 days. Send by **registered post with acknowledgement due** to the drawer's last known address. Address the notice to the **drawer personally**, and (if a company) to the company at its registered office and to the **director(s) responsible** for the conduct of business under s.141.

The notice must clearly state:
- the cheque details (number, date, amount, drawee bank);
- the fact and date of dishonour;
- the reason for dishonour;
- a demand for payment of the cheque amount within 15 days.

A poorly drafted notice can sink your case. Engage a lawyer for this step.

### Step 3: Wait for 15 days from notice receipt
The drawer has 15 days from **receipt** of the notice to pay. If they pay, the matter is closed. If they don't, the offence is complete on the 16th day.

### Step 4: File the complaint — within 30 days
File the complaint under **s.138 read with s.142** of the NI Act before the **Judicial Magistrate of First Class** (or Metropolitan Magistrate) **within 30 days** of the cause of action arising (i.e. the 16th day after the notice).

**Where to file (territorial jurisdiction):** under the post-2015 amendment to s.142(2), the complaint must be filed at:
- the place where the cheque was **delivered for collection** (i.e. where the payee maintains the bank account into which the cheque was deposited), or
- where the cheque was **issued or drawn** by the drawer.

This was a significant change — earlier, payees had wide latitude to choose forum, leading to abuse. The Supreme Court in *Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod v. State of Maharashtra* (2014) had restricted jurisdiction, and the 2015 amendment reset it on the payee-bank-location basis.

### Step 5: The trial — fast-tracked under NI Act s.143
S.138 trials follow a **summary trial procedure** under s.143. The Magistrate may dispense with detailed evidence recording. The trial **shall, as far as practicable, be concluded within 6 months** of filing — though this is a directory standard, often missed in practice.

## Penalty under NI Act s.138

If convicted, the drawer faces:

- **imprisonment up to 2 years**, or
- **fine up to twice the cheque amount**, or
- **both**.

In practice, the courts often impose fines (twice the cheque amount or higher, including compensation to the complainant under BNSS s.395 / earlier CrPC s.357) without imprisonment for first-time offenders. But imprisonment can and does happen.

## Interim compensation — NI Act s.143A (introduced 2018)

This is a powerful tool for the payee. Under **s.143A**, the trial court **may** order the drawer to pay the complainant **interim compensation of up to 20% of the cheque amount** within 60 days of the order. This is paid even before the trial concludes.

If the drawer is later acquitted, the complainant must repay (with interest) — but the bias in favour of the payee in the meantime is significant.

## Deposit on appeal — NI Act s.148

If the drawer is convicted at trial and appeals, the appellate court **shall** (mandatory) order the drawer to deposit a minimum of **20%** of the fine or compensation amount before the appeal is heard. This was introduced in 2018 to deter frivolous appeals.

## Compounding — NI Act s.147

Cheque bounce cases are **compoundable** at any stage. The Supreme Court has actively encouraged settlement, even at appeal. **If the drawer pays the cheque amount with interest, the complainant can withdraw the case and the drawer is acquitted.** Most s.138 cases are settled this way.

The Supreme Court in *Damodar S. Prabhu v. Sayed Babalal H.* (2010) laid down a graded scheme — the later you compound, the higher the cost (5% to 20% of the cheque amount as costs to the State Legal Aid Authority). This was further refined in subsequent decisions.

## Civil parallel — recovery suit

S.138 prosecutes the drawer; it does not by itself recover the money. For larger amounts, you may also need to file a **civil recovery suit / summary suit under Order XXXVII CPC** to actually recover the principal. The two run in parallel, and the criminal court can order compensation, but a civil decree is sometimes needed for execution against larger assets.

## Practical guidance

### If you are the **payee** (received the bounced cheque):
- **Keep originals safe** — the cheque, the return memo, the cheque deposit slip, your bank statement showing the credit reversal.
- **Send the legal notice within 30 days** — this is hard. Don't miss it.
- **Don't accept partial payments without a written settlement.** Partial payment can complicate the criminal case.
- **Consider negotiation first.** Many drawers will pay rather than risk a criminal record.

### If you are the **drawer** (whose cheque bounced):
- **Take notices seriously.** Ignoring a s.138 notice almost guarantees a criminal complaint.
- **If you genuinely owe and can pay, pay within the 15 days.** This makes the whole problem disappear.
- **If you have a real defence**, document it before issuing the cheque was a security cheque, the alleged debt is disputed, etc. Engage a lawyer and reply to the legal notice substantively.
- **Don't go to the bank to "stop payment" hoping that protects you.** Stop-payment after cheque issue, when there was a debt, can still attract s.138 (per *Modi Cements*).

## When companies are involved — NI Act s.141

If the drawer is a company, **the company itself, every director "in charge of and responsible for" the conduct of its business, and other officers** can be prosecuted. Directors are not automatically liable — the complaint must specifically allege their role. The Supreme Court in *S.M.S. Pharmaceuticals v. Neeta Bhalla* (2005) and many subsequent decisions has clarified the standard.
